How far can one photo move?

Apple’s SHARP turns a single photograph into 3D Gaussians you can look around in. We measured where that illusion holds against real photos taken from other positions, and found that SHARP’s own geometry predicts how far each photo can be pushed.

Setup

Render from real camera positions, score against real photos

For each scene we ran SHARP on eight source photos (three of them for the method comparison, which re-renders every baseline), then rendered the scene from the other cameras in the capture and compared each render with the photo actually taken there. Poses come from COLMAP. Predicted scenes are scaled to COLMAP units with one scalar per photo, fit to COLMAP's sparse 3D points. Camera distance is reported as offset ÷ median scene depth, so a 0.10 step on a tabletop and on a room mean the same parallax.

SHARP: Apple's feed-forward predictor, 1.18M Gaussians per photo, 5.7 s on the M4 Pro.
Pixel warp: one opaque splat per pixel at SHARP's own depth, holes filled (Telea). Same geometry, no learned decoder.
Depth Pro warp: the same pixel warp using Apple's Depth Pro depth instead.
Reuse photo: show the source photo unchanged. This is the floor.
Finding 1

SHARP’s edge is mostly perceptual

Give every method the same hole filling and a per-pixel warp at SHARP’s own depth comes within half a decibel of SHARP on PSNR. On LPIPS, a perceptual distance, SHARP is better in every one of the scored pairs, and the gap widens as the camera moves further.

Finding 2

The learned part pays off where the photo never looked

We split every target view into pixels the source photo could see and pixels it could not: newly revealed surfaces and anything outside the original frame. Both regions favour SHARP, but the unseen region is where warping falls apart.

Finding 3

SHARP's geometry predicts how far each photo can go

Before rendering anything we know two things: the requested camera move, and how much of that view SHARP's own geometry says the photo never saw. A small ridge model on those features, tested on scenes it never trained on, explains twice as much of the variation in LPIPS as camera distance alone.

The gain comes from telling photos apart, not directions. Within a single photo, ranking target poses by predicted error is no better than ranking by distance. The practical form is a per-photo safe radius, not a per-direction map. Our test views mostly differ in distance rather than direction, so a capture with varied directions is needed to settle this.

Finding 4

Pixel-level confidence maps

A 2M-parameter UNet sees only what's available at render time: SHARP's render, its opacity, its depth, and the unseen mask. It predicts where the render will disagree with the real photo. We trained with three folds split by scene, so every score comes from scenes the network never saw. It beats the free baseline, the unseen mask alone, most where errors come from geometry: object silhouettes and frame edges. Fine foliage stays hard for both.

Rank scores are averaged over three scene-held-out folds. “Worst 20% pixels” means pixels above the 80th percentile of LPIPS in the training scenes. In the examples below, measured and predicted maps share one scale per row: light is low error, dark red is high. Each example is the median-distance test view for that scene.

Warn with it, don't repair with it

Follow-up

Can generative fill extend the safe range?

The obvious next move is to invent what the photo never saw: a viewer chooses this effect, so plausible content is acceptable. We tested seven fills along 25-frame camera sweeps on five scenes, plus a network trained for the job, and scored both accuracy against the real photo and how much the fill flickers as the camera moves. A side-by-side comparison viewer is available on request.

  • Per-frame generative fill looks best but flickers worst. SDXL inpainting on each frame continues the mat behind a toy truck convincingly, yet changes its invention every frame: frame-to-frame change 19.0 (0–255) inside unseen areas, against 3.1 for raw SHARP, which is static but empty.
  • Predicting the fill once in 3D removes the flicker. A learned background layer — one hidden colour and depth behind every source pixel, rendered with the scene — is the steadiest fill measured (7.3), because a 3D prediction cannot change when the camera moves.
  • But its content still loses to SHARP. On held-out scenes, error in unseen regions is 0.163 for the layer against 0.144 for SHARP's own stretched pixels (perceptual 0.414 vs 0.362). Where the layer paints, it is worse than the smear it replaces in 88% of pairs.
  • More data does not fix it. The same model trained on 2,000 vs 7,012 pairs scores 0.164 vs 0.163, with an identical win rate. The limit is the from-scratch appearance model, not the dataset — so scaling this architecture on a cluster would waste the allocation. The compute belongs on adapting a large pretrained video or multi-view diffusion prior.
  • Two structural facts this exposed. SHARP covers 75% of unseen pixels with opaque stretched content, so a benchmark that scores those pixels is largely scoring a smear, and a fair protocol should treat them as unknown rather than as content. The other 25% is where SHARP paints nothing at all. Geometrically the unseen region splits differently: 65% of it lies beyond the source photo's frame (SHARP stretches splats over most of that, just wrongly), 34% is background a foreground object was covering, and only 1.2% is a foreground object's own hidden surface. So effort belongs in extending past the frame and completing background behind objects; regenerating the far side of objects would address almost nothing at this kind of camera motion, though an orbit around a hero object would change that balance.
Caveats and next steps

What this does and doesn't show yet

  • Held-out views come from real captures, so ground truth is real, but LLFF's nearest neighbours are already 2–5° apart. We can't measure the sub-degree regime where spatial-photo UIs operate.
  • Scale is fit per photo from COLMAP points. Horns (glass, railings) has a 27% median depth-ratio error, which inflates its error for every method equally.
  • Most remaining prediction error is a constant offset per scene: flower is under-predicted by 0.15 LPIPS, orchids over-predicted by 0.12. Ranking within a scene stays strong (ρ 0.86–0.97). Calibrating each photo from one nearby real view, a stand-in for a phone's second lens, lifts rank correlation from 0.81 to 0.89 and safe-radius ρ from 0.84 to 0.89. The same anchor applied to a camera-distance model fails (R² −0.13).
  • PSNR rewards blur. Before we gave SHARP the same hole fill, smooth inpainting of out-of-frame regions let the pixel warp tie it on PSNR while looking clearly worse, which is why LPIPS is the headline metric. Every number here uses identical filling for all methods.
  • Prior art to position against. US patent 12,051,150 (priority Dec 2021) scores whether a photo suits a single-image 3D effect by counting non-occluded pixels in rendered target views, which is close to our unseen-area feature used as a yes/no score. Predictive Photometric Uncertainty in Gaussian Splatting (arXiv 2603.22786, 2026) estimates per-pixel uncertainty, but for scenes optimised from many views. UniSHARP (arXiv 2606.07514, 2026) extends SHARP to fisheye and panoramic cameras without estimating quality. The open contributions here: validation against real held-out photos, a continuous per-photo safe radius, per-direction ranking, pixel-level confidence for feed-forward single-image models, and evidence that it transfers across renderers.
  • Next: a capture with dense, directionally varied cameras around each source (a phone orbit, not a LLFF grid) to test per-direction prediction. A fine-tune that trades SHARP's hallucinated detail for predicted confidence. A user study pairing the predicted safe radius with perceived quality.
